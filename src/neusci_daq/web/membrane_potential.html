Exploring the membrane potential

Why is the inside of a resting neuron about 70 mV below the outside? Watch that voltage build up from individual ions, then explain it with the equations below.

You'll be able to
  • explain how a tiny separation of charge makes the membrane potential (Q = CmVm)
  • predict the resting potential from concentrations and conductances, with the Nernst, chord conductance and GHK equations
  • explain why no net current flows at rest even though ions keep crossing

Theory

What sets the membrane potential

The voltage is a small charge \(Q\) held on the membrane capacitor, \(Q = C_m V_m\). Every ion that can cross pulls the membrane toward its own Nernst potential, and the more easily it crosses, the harder it pulls. The voltage settles where those pulls balance: where the currents of all the ions add up to zero. Two formulas describe that balance. The chord conductance formula treats each ion as a battery behind a resistor; the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz (GHK) equation starts from ions diffusing and drifting through the membrane.

Charge and capacitance
\[ Q = C_m\,V_m \]
Chord conductance
\[ V_m = \frac{g_\mathrm{K}E_\mathrm{K} + g_\mathrm{Na}E_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{Cl}E_\mathrm{Cl}}{g_\mathrm{K} + g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{Cl}} \]
Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz
\[ V_m = \frac{RT}{F}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{P_\mathrm{K}[\mathrm{K}^+]_o + P_\mathrm{Na}[\mathrm{Na}^+]_o + P_\mathrm{Cl}[\mathrm{Cl}^-]_i}{P_\mathrm{K}[\mathrm{K}^+]_i + P_\mathrm{Na}[\mathrm{Na}^+]_i + P_\mathrm{Cl}[\mathrm{Cl}^-]_o}\right) \]

The voltage is a charge separation

A membrane potential means a little extra negative charge on the inside face of the membrane and an equal extra positive charge on the outside face. The membrane is a capacitor, and the charge \(Q\) on each side and the voltage are tied by its capacitance:

\[ Q = C_m\,V_m \]

Cell membranes have \(C_m \approx 1\ \mu\text{F/cm}^2\). At −70 mV that is about \(7\times10^{-8}\) C/cm², or \(4\times10^{11}\) ions per cm². In a cell body 30 µm across, that is about 1 in 100,000 of the ions inside, so setting up the voltage barely changes the concentrations.

Where the charge sits

Not on a metal plate. The extra ions are in the solution, pulled toward the membrane by the opposite charge across it and spread back out by diffusion. The balance gives a layer whose charge density falls off exponentially with distance \(x\) from the membrane, and \(Q\) is the area under it:

\[ \rho(x) = \rho_0\,e^{-x/\lambda_D}, \qquad Q = \int_0^\infty \rho\,dx = \rho_0\,\lambda_D \]

\(\lambda_D\) is the Debye length. It is shorter in saltier solutions, about 0.8 nm at 150 mM, so in a real cell each layer is only a few ions thick. Here it is 40 px so you can see it. In the charge and potential chart, the curve on each side is this exponential, scaled to the bars, and the shaded area under it is \(Q\), counted out to \(3\lambda_D\), which holds 95% of the layer's charge.

What sets the capacitance

A capacitor stores more charge per volt when its plates are close together and the gap between them is easy to polarize: \(C = \varepsilon/d\). The membrane's oily core is about 4 nm thick with a low dielectric constant (about 2, against water's 80), which gives roughly 0.5–1 µF/cm². Each diffuse layer is also a capacitor, with a gap of \(\lambda_D\) filled with water, so its capacitance is about 100 times larger. The three sit in series, and the smallest capacitance takes most of the voltage: nearly all of \(V_m\) drops across the membrane itself, as the potential line in the chart shows.

Show the derivation

This shows where the exponential layer and \(C = \varepsilon/d\) come from. It uses two ideas from intro physics and chemistry: charge makes voltage, and ions spread out according to the Boltzmann distribution.

\(\phi(x)\)
voltage in the solution at distance \(x\) from the membrane, measured from the far-away bulk solution
\(\rho(x)\)
net charge per volume at that spot; more cations than anions makes it positive
\(c_s^\infty,\ z_s\)
concentration of ion \(s\) far from the membrane, and its charge (+1 for K⁺, −1 for Cl⁻)
\(\varepsilon_w,\ \varepsilon_m\)
permittivity of water and of the membrane's oily core: how much each one weakens an electric field. Water's is about 40 times larger.
\(d\)
thickness of the membrane core, about 4 nm

1. Charge bends the voltage (Poisson's equation). Extra positive charge makes the voltage curve downward, like the top of a hill; extra negative charge makes it curve upward, like a valley. In water, with its large \(\varepsilon_w\), the same charge bends it less.

\[ \frac{d^2\phi}{dx^2} = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_w} \]

2. Ions follow the voltage (Boltzmann distribution). A cation is less likely to be found where the voltage is high, much as air is thinner at the top of a mountain:

\[ c_s(x) = c_s^\infty\,e^{-z_s F\phi/RT} \]

When the voltage is small next to \(RT/F\) (about 25 mV), the exponential is close to a straight line, \(e^{-y} \approx 1 - y\). The bulk solution is neutral, so the "1" terms cancel when the charges are added up. What is left is a net charge proportional to the voltage, with the opposite sign:

\[ \rho \approx -\frac{\varepsilon_w}{\lambda_D^2}\,\phi, \qquad \lambda_D = \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_w RT}{F^2 \sum_s z_s^2 c_s^\infty}} \]

In words: wherever the voltage is raised, the ions rearrange to cancel it. The Debye length \(\lambda_D\) says how far that takes. Saltier solutions have more ions to do the cancelling, so \(\lambda_D\) is shorter.

3. Put steps 1 and 2 together. The voltage must be a function whose second derivative is proportional to itself, which is an exponential. The one that dies away into the bulk is

\[ \frac{d^2\phi}{dx^2} = \frac{\phi}{\lambda_D^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \phi = \phi_0\,e^{-x/\lambda_D}, \qquad \rho = \rho_0\,e^{-x/\lambda_D} \]

So the voltage and the extra charge both drop by a factor of \(e\) (about 2.7) for every Debye length away from the membrane. That is the curve drawn on the charge chart.

4. Add up the charge. The area under an exponential is its height times its decay length:

\[ Q = \int_0^\infty \rho\,dx = \rho_0\,\lambda_D = -\frac{\varepsilon_w}{\lambda_D}\,\phi_0 \]

The layer's charge is proportional to the voltage across it, which is what a capacitor does. Each layer acts like a capacitor with its plates \(\lambda_D\) apart: \(C_D = \varepsilon_w/\lambda_D\).

5. The membrane itself. There are no free ions in the oily core, so the electric field there is constant and the voltage drops in a straight line; that is the steep straight section of the potential line in the chart. With charge \(Q\) on each face and thickness \(d\):

\[ C_m = \frac{\varepsilon_m}{d} \]

A thin core with a small \(\varepsilon_m\) makes this the smallest of the three capacitances.

6. Put them in a row. From inside to outside, the voltage drops across the inner layer, then the membrane, then the outer layer. For capacitors in a row (in series), the inverses add:

\[ \frac{1}{C} = \frac{1}{C_m} + \frac{2}{C_D} \]

With real numbers, \(C_m \approx 0.4\text{–}1\ \mu\text{F/cm}^2\) and \(C_D \approx 90\ \mu\text{F/cm}^2\). The layers' term is about 100 times smaller, so \(C \approx C_m\) and \(Q \approx C_m V_m\). That is why the textbook value of about 1 µF/cm² is the membrane's own capacitance.

Charge moves, voltage follows

\(V_m\) changes only when charge crosses the membrane, through channels or pumps. Any net current charges or discharges the capacitor:

\[ I_\text{net} = C_m\,\frac{dV_m}{dt} \]

This is the link to the steady-state tab. At rest the voltage is steady, so the net current is zero; that is the condition both the chord conductance and GHK formulas solve. Away from rest, net current flows until the capacitor reaches the new voltage, with time constant \(\tau = C_m/g\) (see the RC membrane page). Capacitance sets how much charge has to move, and so how long it takes, but not where \(V_m\) ends up.

Where it falls short

  • The plain exponential is the linearized (Debye–Hückel) layer. It holds while the voltage across each layer is small next to \(RT/F \approx 25\) mV, as it is here; larger layer voltages make the layer steeper near the membrane.
  • Real membranes also carry fixed charge on their lipid head groups and proteins, which gathers layers of its own and shifts the voltage that channels sense.

At steady state \(V_m\) has stopped changing, so no net current is charging the membrane capacitor: the ions' currents add up to zero. Two formulas find the voltage where that happens.

Ohm's law, one ion at a time

Ohm's law says the current through a resistor is the voltage across it divided by its resistance. Written with conductance \(g = 1/R\), which says how easily current flows, it is

\[ I = \frac{V}{R} = g\,V \]

An open ion channel is a resistor, with one difference: the ion's concentration gradient acts like a battery in series with it. The battery's voltage is the ion's Nernst potential \(E_s\), the voltage at which that ion is in equilibrium and carries no net current. So the voltage that drives the current is not \(V_m\) itself but how far \(V_m\) is from \(E_s\):

\[ I_s = g_s\,(V_m - E_s) \]

This is Ohm's law with \(V\) replaced by the driving force \(V_m - E_s\). The conductance \(g_s\) is set by how many open channels the ion has. At \(V_m = E_s\) the current is zero; the farther \(V_m\) is from \(E_s\), the larger it is, and its sign says which way the ion moves (positive is outward). For K⁺ in a resting neuron, \(V_m \approx -70\) mV and \(E_\mathrm{K} \approx -90\) mV, so the driving force is +20 mV and K⁺ flows out.

In circuit terms each ion is a battery and a resistor, and all of them sit side by side (in parallel) across the membrane capacitor.

Where the currents balance

At rest no net current is charging the capacitor, so the ionic currents cancel. Solving for \(V_m\) gives an average of the Nernst potentials:

\[ V_m = \frac{g_\mathrm{K}E_\mathrm{K} + g_\mathrm{Na}E_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{Cl}E_\mathrm{Cl}}{g_\mathrm{K} + g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{Cl}} \]

Each Nernst potential is weighted by its ion's share of the total conductance. If one ion has nearly all the conductance, \(V_m\) sits at its Nernst potential.

Show the derivation

1. At rest the currents add up to zero: whatever charge flows in through one ion's channels flows out through another's.

\[ g_\mathrm{K}(V_m - E_\mathrm{K}) + g_\mathrm{Na}(V_m - E_\mathrm{Na}) + g_\mathrm{Cl}(V_m - E_\mathrm{Cl}) = 0 \]

2. Expand and collect the \(V_m\) terms on one side:

\[ V_m\,(g_\mathrm{K} + g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{Cl}) = g_\mathrm{K}E_\mathrm{K} + g_\mathrm{Na}E_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{Cl}E_\mathrm{Cl} \]

3. Divide by the total conductance to get the chord conductance formula above.

Where it falls short

  • A channel is not a fixed resistor, so Ohm's law with a constant \(g_s\) is only an approximation. Even an always-open channel passes current more easily in the direction that has more ions to carry it, so its current–voltage curve bends, and \(g_s\) changes with voltage and with concentrations. Raise [K⁺]ₒ and \(g_\mathrm{K}\) should rise too; the formula cannot say by how much.
  • The Nernst potentials are inputs. The formula takes \(E_s\) as given, so it says nothing about how a concentration gradient makes a voltage.
  • Zero net current is not zero flux. At rest K⁺ keeps leaking out and Na⁺ keeps leaking in, each a steady current that the others cancel. Over time the gradients run down unless the Na⁺/K⁺ pump restores them, and the pump's own current (3 charges out for every 2 in) is not in the formula.
  • It says where \(V_m\) ends up, not how fast it gets there. That is set by the membrane capacitance; see the RC membrane page.

Start from the ions, not the circuit

GHK asks what each ion does inside the membrane. It diffuses down its concentration gradient and drifts in the electric field, which is exactly what the dots in the simulation do. Two assumptions make this solvable: the electric field is constant across the membrane, so the potential falls in a straight line from one face to the other, and each ion crosses independently, without binding or interacting with the others.

What comes out: a current for each ion

Solving gives each ion's current at any voltage, the GHK current equation, with \(u_s = z_s F V_m / RT\):

\[ I_s = P_s\,z_s^2\,\frac{F^2 V_m}{RT}\;\frac{[S]_i - [S]_o\,e^{-u_s}}{1 - e^{-u_s}} \]

Two things differ from the chord picture. The membrane property is the permeability \(P_s\), and concentrations enter the current directly, not only through \(E_s\). And the current is not a straight line: it is zero at \(E_s\) and bends, carrying more current in the direction that has more ions to carry it. The coloured curves in the calculator's chart are these currents.

Where they balance

Add the currents of all the ions and set the sum to zero. For ions with charge ±1 the exponential factors are the same for every ion, so \(V_m\) can be solved for exactly:

\[ V_m = \frac{RT}{F}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{P_\mathrm{K}[\mathrm{K}^+]_o + P_\mathrm{Na}[\mathrm{Na}^+]_o + P_\mathrm{Cl}[\mathrm{Cl}^-]_i}{P_\mathrm{K}[\mathrm{K}^+]_i + P_\mathrm{Na}[\mathrm{Na}^+]_i + P_\mathrm{Cl}[\mathrm{Cl}^-]_o}\right) \]

Cl⁻ appears with inside and outside swapped because its charge is negative. With only one ion permeant, everything else cancels and this is the Nernst equation.

Show the full derivation
\(R, T, F\)
gas constant, absolute temperature, Faraday constant; \(RT/F \approx 25.7\) mV at 25 °C
\(z_s\)
valence of ion \(s\) (+1 for K⁺ and Na⁺, −1 for Cl⁻)
\(c_s(x)\)
concentration of ion \(s\) at depth \(x\) in the membrane, \(0 \le x \le d\), inside face at \(x=0\)
\(D_s,\ \beta_s,\ d\)
diffusion coefficient in the membrane, membrane/water partition coefficient, membrane thickness
\(P_s\)
permeability, \(P_s = D_s\beta_s/d\); only the ratios between ions matter for \(V_m\)
\(J_s\)
flux of ion \(s\), positive outward (mol per area per time)

1. Nernst–Planck: each ion moves by diffusion plus drift in the field.

\[ J_s = -D_s\left(\frac{dc_s}{dx} + \frac{z_s F}{RT}\,c_s\,\frac{d\phi}{dx}\right) \]

2. Constant field: with \(\phi(0)=V_m\) and \(\phi(d)=0\), \(d\phi/dx = -V_m/d\), so

\[ J_s = -D_s\left(\frac{dc_s}{dx} - \frac{u_s}{d}\,c_s\right) \]

3. At steady state \(J_s\) is the same at every depth. Multiply by \(e^{-u_s x/d}\), integrate from 0 to \(d\) with \(c_s(0)=\beta_s[S]_i\) and \(c_s(d)=\beta_s[S]_o\), and solve for the flux:

\[ J_s = P_s\,u_s\,\frac{[S]_i - [S]_o\,e^{-u_s}}{1 - e^{-u_s}} \]

4. Zero current: with \(v = FV_m/RT\), \(u_s = v\) for cations and \(-v\) for anions. Setting \(\sum_s z_s J_s = 0\) and cancelling the common factor \(v/(1-e^{-v})\):

\[ \sum_{\text{cat}} P_s[S]_i + \sum_{\text{an}} P_s[S]_o \;=\; e^{-v}\Big(\sum_{\text{cat}} P_s[S]_o + \sum_{\text{an}} P_s[S]_i\Big) \]

Solving for \(v\) and multiplying by \(RT/F\) gives the GHK voltage equation.

Where it falls short

  • Real channels are not uniform slabs. Ions bind in the selectivity filter, move single file and can saturate the channel, so permeability ratios measured at one set of concentrations may not hold at another.
  • The voltage equation needs every permeant ion to have the same size of charge. With Ca²⁺ in the mix, you go back to the current equation and find the zero crossing numerically.
  • Like the chord formula, it describes zero net current, not zero flux, and it leaves out the pump's current.

The general picture

  • Each concentration gradient is a battery. An ion's Nernst potential is the voltage at which its drift in the field exactly balances its diffusion down the gradient.
  • Each permeant ion pulls \(V_m\) toward its own battery, with a strength set by how easily it crosses.
  • The membrane is a capacitor, so very little charge sets the voltage: about 1 ion in 100,000 near the membrane. The thin layers in the charge profile are that charge, and the bulk concentrations barely change.
  • \(V_m\) settles where the inward and outward currents cancel. That is a steady state, not an equilibrium: the ions keep leaking, and the pump pays to keep the gradients.

Both formulas say exactly this. They differ only in how each ion's current depends on voltage.

Same answer, different things held fixed

Take each ion's GHK current at the resting potential and divide by its driving force: that is its chord conductance, and the chord formula with those conductances gives exactly the GHK voltage. Both describe the same zero crossing. They part when something changes. Raise [K⁺]ₒ: GHK holds \(P_\mathrm{K}\) fixed, so the extra K⁺ outside also makes the K⁺ current larger, while the chord formula holds \(g_\mathrm{K}\) fixed and only moves \(E_\mathrm{K}\). For K⁺ and Na⁺ over normal ranges the difference is small, which is why the Hodgkin–Huxley model can use fixed conductances. For Ca²⁺, whose gradient is about 10,000-fold, the current is so curved that only GHK will do.
