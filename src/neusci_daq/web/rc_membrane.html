Charging a membrane: the RC circuit and the ions

A membrane is a capacitor (the thin lipid bilayer) in parallel with resistors (the ion channels), each in series with a battery (that ion's Nernst potential). When something changes, current first goes into charging the capacitor; only once the voltage has moved to its new value does all of the current flow around through the resistors. The top half shows this with a circuit; the bottom half does the same thing with ions, using the particle model from the Membrane potential page.

Current sets how fast the voltage changes

The voltage across a capacitor is the charge on it divided by its capacitance. So the only way to change the voltage is to move charge onto or off the plates, and the current into the capacitor is how fast that charge moves:

\[ Q = C\,V \quad\Rightarrow\quad I_C = \frac{dQ}{dt} = C\,\frac{dV}{dt} \]

No charge builds up on the wires, so whatever the channels carry across must come off the capacitor (Kirchhoff's current law). The voltage changes at a rate set by the net channel current:

\[ I_C + I_\mathrm{Na} + I_\mathrm{K} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{dV}{dt} = -\,\frac{I_\mathrm{Na} + I_\mathrm{K}}{C} \]

A quick number

With currents in µA/cm² and C in µF/cm², the rate comes out in mV/ms: 100 µA/cm² into 1 µF/cm² moves V at 100 mV/ms. At the default settings, opening the Na⁺ channels at rest gives \(I_\mathrm{Na} = 0.7 \times (-86 - 60) \approx -102\) µA/cm² while \(I_\mathrm{K} = 0\), so V starts climbing at about 100 mV/ms.

Why it slows down

Each channel current is \(g\,(V - E)\), so the currents shrink as V nears the voltage where they cancel, \(V_\infty\). The rate shrinks with them:

\[ \frac{dV}{dt} = -\,\frac{g_\mathrm{Na}(V - E_\mathrm{Na}) + g_\mathrm{K}(V - E_\mathrm{K})}{C} = \frac{V_\infty - V}{\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{C}{g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{K}} \]

\(V_\infty\) is the voltage where the channel currents cancel, so nothing more charges the capacitor. Setting \(dV/dt = 0\):

\[ g_\mathrm{Na}(V_\infty - E_\mathrm{Na}) + g_\mathrm{K}(V_\infty - E_\mathrm{K}) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad V_\infty = \frac{g_\mathrm{Na}E_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{K}E_\mathrm{K}}{g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{K}} \]

The GHK equation answers the same question, where the currents add to zero, for real channels. Real channels, and the ones in the particle model, are not ohmic: their currents follow the GHK current equation, and the voltage at which those currents cancel is the GHK voltage. So for a membrane, \(V_\infty\) is the solution of the GHK equation:

\[ V_\infty = \frac{RT}{F}\,\ln\frac{P_\mathrm{K}[\mathrm{K}^+]_o + P_\mathrm{Na}[\mathrm{Na}^+]_o}{P_\mathrm{K}[\mathrm{K}^+]_i + P_\mathrm{Na}[\mathrm{Na}^+]_i} \]

The two formulas agree when each g is the channel's chord conductance at that voltage, \(g = I/(V - E)\) (see the Membrane potential page). The circuit uses fixed g instead, so its \(V_\infty\) is close to, but not exactly, the GHK value; in the particle model the voltage settles on the GHK line.

A rate proportional to the distance still to go gives an exponential approach:

\[ V(t) = V_\infty + \left(V_0 - V_\infty\right) e^{-t/\tau} \]

At the defaults \(\tau = 1/(0.7 + 0.5) \approx 0.83\) ms: V covers about 63% of the way in one τ and 95% in three.

In the particle model

The same rule is a count. Each net ion that crosses adds one charge to the membrane, which moves V by a fixed step: about 2 mV at the default Membrane C (the bilayer's capacitance in series with the charge layers in the solution). On the Ions moved chart, "on membrane" is that net count, and the voltage trace follows its shape.

What to look for

  • Close the switch (or open the Na⁺ channels). At first almost all the Na⁺ current goes into the capacitor: the plates fill, and in the particle model Na⁺ ions pile up as positive charge just inside the membrane.
  • As the voltage rises, the K⁺ branch carries more and more current outward and the Na⁺ branch less inward, because both are now further from (or closer to) their batteries.
  • Once the voltage stops changing, the capacitor current is zero. Current now goes all the way around: in through Na⁺, out through K⁺. The charge on the membrane is constant, yet ions keep crossing.
  • Open the switch again and the capacitor discharges through the K⁺ branch alone, more slowly, because there is less conductance.

What it simplifies

  • The circuit uses linear (ohmic) channels. Real channels, and the particle model, follow the GHK current equation, so the particle voltage settles at the GHK value, close to but not exactly the circuit's conductance-weighted average.
  • The particle model is the one on the Membrane potential page with Cl⁻ folded into the impermeant anions, no pump, and gated Na⁺ channels added. Both sides are held at fixed concentrations far from the membrane.
  • Ion counts are tiny compared with a real cell, so the particle traces are noisy; the voltage is averaged over many steps. Time is in simulation steps, not milliseconds.
  • Real Na⁺ channels open with voltage and then inactivate, which gives an action potential. Here they only open and close when you tell them to.
