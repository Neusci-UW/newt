Membrane potential, ion by ion

Each dot is an ion doing a random walk. Ions cross only through channels selective for them, and every ion feels the electric field made by all the others. Nothing here is told the Nernst or GHK equation, yet the voltage settles onto those values, and the excess charge gathers in thin layers on the two faces of the membrane, like charge on a capacitor.

The Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation

The GHK voltage equation gives the membrane potential at which several permeant ions together carry zero net current. It reduces to the Nernst equation when only one ion can cross.

\[ \begin{aligned} V_m &\;=\; \frac{RT}{F}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{P_\mathrm{K}[\mathrm{K}^+]_o + P_\mathrm{Na}[\mathrm{Na}^+]_o + P_\mathrm{Cl}[\mathrm{Cl}^-]_i}{P_\mathrm{K}[\mathrm{K}^+]_i + P_\mathrm{Na}[\mathrm{Na}^+]_i + P_\mathrm{Cl}[\mathrm{Cl}^-]_o}\right) \\[1.5ex] &\;=\; \frac{g_\mathrm{K}E_\mathrm{K} + g_\mathrm{Na}E_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{Cl}E_\mathrm{Cl}}{g_\mathrm{K} + g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{Cl}} \end{aligned} \]

Definitions

\(V_m\)
membrane potential, \(\phi_{\text{in}} - \phi_{\text{out}}\)
\(R, T, F\)
gas constant, absolute temperature, Faraday constant; \(RT/F \approx 25.7\) mV at 25 °C
\(z_s\)
valence of ion \(s\) (+1 for K⁺ and Na⁺, −1 for Cl⁻)
\(c_s(x)\)
concentration of ion \(s\) at depth \(x\) in the membrane, \(0 \le x \le d\), inside face at \(x=0\)
\([S]_i, [S]_o\)
bulk concentrations inside and outside
\(D_s,\ \beta_s,\ d\)
diffusion coefficient in the membrane, membrane/water partition coefficient, membrane thickness
\(P_s\)
permeability, \(P_s = D_s\beta_s/d\) (units of velocity); only the ratios between ions matter for \(V_m\)
\(J_s\)
flux of ion \(s\), positive outward (mol per area per time)
\(u_s\)
reduced voltage, \(u_s = z_s F V_m / RT\)

Assumptions

(1) Steady state, with each ion crossing independently. (2) The electric field is constant through the membrane, so \(\phi\) falls linearly from \(V_m\) at the inside face to 0 at the outside face. (3) The concentration just inside each face is the bulk concentration times \(\beta_s\).

1. Nernst–Planck flux

Each ion moves by diffusion down its concentration gradient plus drift in the electric field:

\[ J_s = -D_s\left(\frac{dc_s}{dx} + \frac{z_s F}{RT}\,c_s\,\frac{d\phi}{dx}\right) \]

2. Apply the constant field

With \(\phi(0)=V_m\) and \(\phi(d)=0\), the slope is \(d\phi/dx = -V_m/d\), so

\[ J_s = -D_s\left(\frac{dc_s}{dx} - \frac{u_s}{d}\,c_s\right) \]

3. Integrate across the membrane

At steady state \(J_s\) is the same at every \(x\). Multiply by the integrating factor \(e^{-u_s x/d}\):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\!\left(c_s\,e^{-u_s x/d}\right) = -\frac{J_s}{D_s}\,e^{-u_s x/d} \]

Integrate from \(x=0\) to \(x=d\), using the boundary values \(c_s(0)=\beta_s[S]_i\) and \(c_s(d)=\beta_s[S]_o\):

\[ \beta_s[S]_o\,e^{-u_s} - \beta_s[S]_i = \frac{J_s\,d}{D_s\,u_s}\left(e^{-u_s}-1\right) \]

Solving for the flux gives the GHK flux equation:

\[ J_s = P_s\,u_s\,\frac{[S]_i - [S]_o\,e^{-u_s}}{1 - e^{-u_s}} \]

4. Set the net current to zero

The electric current is \(I = F\sum_s z_s J_s\). Write \(v = FV_m/RT\). For cations \(u_s = v\); for anions \(u_s = -v\), and rearranging the anion term with \(1-e^{v} = -e^{v}(1-e^{-v})\) gives

\[ z_s J_s = \begin{cases} P_s\,v\,\dfrac{[S]_i - [S]_o e^{-v}}{1-e^{-v}} & \text{cations} \\[2.2ex] P_s\,v\,\dfrac{[S]_o - [S]_i e^{-v}}{1-e^{-v}} & \text{anions} \end{cases} \]

Setting \(\sum_s z_s J_s = 0\) and cancelling the common factor \(v/(1-e^{-v})\):

\[ \sum_{\text{cat}} P_s[S]_i + \sum_{\text{an}} P_s[S]_o \;=\; e^{-v}\Big(\sum_{\text{cat}} P_s[S]_o + \sum_{\text{an}} P_s[S]_i\Big) \]

Solving for \(v\) and multiplying by \(RT/F\) gives the GHK voltage equation above. Anion concentrations appear with inside and outside swapped because their charge is negative.

5. One permeant ion gives Nernst

If only K⁺ crosses, the other terms vanish and \(V_m = \frac{RT}{F}\ln\frac{[\mathrm{K}^+]_o}{[\mathrm{K}^+]_i} = E_\mathrm{K}\). With several ions, \(V_m\) is a permeability-weighted compromise between their Nernst potentials, pulled toward the most permeant ion.

6. The same answer as a weighted average: the chord conductance formula

Each ion's GHK current is zero at its own Nernst potential \(E_s\), so at any voltage it can be written as a conductance times a driving force:

\[ I_s = g_s\,(V - E_s), \qquad g_s = \frac{I_s(V)}{V - E_s} \]

\(g_s\) is the chord conductance: the slope of the straight line (the chord) from \((E_s, 0)\) to the GHK current curve at \(V\). At the resting potential the currents cancel, so

\[ \sum_s g_s\,(V_m - E_s) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad V_m = \frac{g_\mathrm{K}E_\mathrm{K} + g_\mathrm{Na}E_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{Cl}E_\mathrm{Cl}}{g_\mathrm{K} + g_\mathrm{Na} + g_\mathrm{Cl}} \]

With the chord conductances taken at \(V_m\), this is exactly the GHK potential: the same condition, zero net current, written another way. What differs is what is held fixed. GHK currents curve, so each \(g_s\) changes with voltage and with concentrations, and the chord formula with fixed conductances is a straight-line approximation. Change a concentration and the two part: GHK with fixed permeabilities is the better guide when concentrations change a lot, and essential for Ca²⁺, whose 10,000-fold gradient makes its current strongly curved. Fixed conductances work well for Na⁺ and K⁺ during an action potential, which is why the Hodgkin–Huxley model uses them. The toggle on the calculator switches between the two.

How the simulation maps onto this

Inside each channel the potential falls linearly across the membrane, which is assumption 2. Permeability is proportional to the number of channels for that ion, since every channel is always open. The simulation never uses the formula to move ions; it uses the formula only to draw the GHK reference line.

What the model does

  • Every ion takes random Brownian steps, smaller inside channels than in the bulk solutions (the step size changes smoothly near the membrane, with the drift correction that keeps equilibrium exact). On top of that it drifts in the local electric field, which comes from all charges to its left (a 1D Poisson solve each step, with the bath electrode at the right wall as ground).
  • The membrane has a much lower dielectric constant than water, so the same charge makes a much steeper field inside it. That is why most of the voltage drops across the membrane.
  • Excess charge is pulled toward the membrane by its partner on the other side and spread out by diffusion. The balance gives the diffuse layers whose width is the Debye length.
  • With one permeable ion, the voltage between the two bulk solutions stops at that ion's Nernst potential. With several, it settles near the GHK value.
  • The simulated patch wraps top to bottom, as a piece of a much longer membrane, and its length is set by the Ion count slider. Capacitance is set per length of membrane, so the voltage keeps its meaning as the patch grows or shrinks; a longer patch averages over more ions, so the graphs are steadier.

What it simplifies

  • Fields only vary across the membrane (left to right). Ions do not collide, and there is no field along the membrane.
  • By default both sides are infinite reservoirs: the far bulk on each side is held at fixed concentrations, the standard simplification for a real cell, where charging the membrane moves only about 1 ion in 100,000. Untick the reservoir boxes to see what happens when a few mV per ion does deplete a finite cell.
  • Real diffuse layers are about 1 nm thick. Here they are tens of pixels, so you can see them.
  • All channels are leak channels, always open. There are no voltage-gated channels or action potentials here; each ion's permeability is its number of channels.
  • It is 2D. Ions crowd slightly at busy channel mouths and the charge layers carry a few mV, so GHK is a close but not exact match when several ions are permeant.
