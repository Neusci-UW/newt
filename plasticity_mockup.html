Learning goals high-yield first

  • NMDA receptors are coincidence detectors: they pass Ca²⁺ only when glutamate is bound and Post is depolarized enough to push Mg²⁺ out of the pore.
  • LTP vs LTD come down to Ca²⁺: a lot of Ca²⁺ fast (100 Hz tetanus, or pairing) turns on CaMKII and adds AMPA receptors (LTP). A little Ca²⁺ for a long time (1 Hz for 15 min) turns on calcineurin, a phosphatase, which takes AMPA receptors out (LTD).
  • Input specificity: only the stimulated spine changes; its neighbour on the same dendrite does not, because Ca²⁺ stays inside the spine head.
  • Blocking NMDA receptors (APV) prevents LTP and LTD but does not change the ordinary EPSP, which runs through AMPA receptors.
  • Lower-yield, kept short: the EPSP is biggest at the spine head and is smaller and slower at the soma (the RC low-pass from lesson 2). How much the neck itself adds is still argued about; the page says so.

Model (one model behind all three parts)

  • Four compartments, each an RC like lesson 2: spine head A (stimulated), spine head B (neighbour), a dendrite segment, the soma. Each neck is a resistor from its head to the dendrite (slider, 50 to 1000 MΩ), the dendrite has a resistor to the soma. Units mV, ms, nS, pA, pF; exponential Euler at 25 µs, so it is stable at every neck setting and cheap (a 150 ms run is 6,000 steps).
  • Receptors in head A use the Synapse page's count model: AMPA (rise 0.2, decay 2 ms) and NMDA (rise 3, decay 60 ms) times mgFree(V). LTP and LTD change the AMPA count; LTP also enlarges the head (drawn; capacitance kept the same).
  • Ca²⁺ in each head: in through NMDA receptors (and a little through voltage-gated channels), out by pumps and out through the neck into the dendrite. A thinner neck keeps it in longer.
  • Plasticity rule (two enzymes, pre-med words): CaMKII grows with the time Ca²⁺ spends above a high level; calcineurin is half-on at a low level but slow, so it builds up only over many pulses. Change in AMPA count = CaMKII part − calcineurin part, capped at 40–220 %. Part 3 runs the protocols at 1 ms steps off-screen (15 min of 1 Hz is under 20 ms of work), then plays the result.

1 · Spine and dendrite: where the EPSP is biggest

The Synapse page treats Post as one point. Here the synapse sits on a spine head, joined to the dendrite by a thin neck. Each part charges like the RC circuit of lesson 2, and the resistors between them filter the EPSP on its way to the soma.

Post: voltage at three places

The same EPSPs, each scaled to its own peak

What to look for: the EPSP in the spine head is several times bigger than at the soma, and the soma's EPSP peaks later and lasts longer (a low-pass filter). A thinner neck makes the head's EPSP bigger but barely changes the soma's. How spatial and temporal summation work at the soma is in the Summation lesson.

2 · Ca²⁺ in the spine: NMDA receptors need two things at once

NMDA receptors open when glutamate binds, but at rest Mg²⁺ sits in the pore and blocks it. Depolarizing Post pushes the Mg²⁺ out. Only with both does much Ca²⁺ flow in, and the narrow neck keeps that Ca²⁺ in its own spine.

Post: voltage in head A and the dendrite

Ca²⁺ in each spine head

What to look for: glutamate alone gives a small Ca²⁺ rise; depolarizing alone gives almost none; together they give many times more than the two added up. Spine B, on the same dendrite and depolarized just as much, gets almost no Ca²⁺. Take the Mg²⁺ out and glutamate alone is enough.

3 · LTP and LTD: the test pulse before and after

A small test pulse every 30 s measures the EPSP. Then one induction protocol, then the test pulses again for an hour. Spine B gets the test pulses but not the protocol, so it is the control.

Ca²⁺ in head A during induction

Post: EPSP size, % of the start

Post: test EPSP at the soma, before and after

What to look for: the tetanus drives Ca²⁺ above the CaMKII level and the EPSP stays bigger for the whole hour (the first few minutes are extra big: post-tetanic potentiation, a presynaptic effect that fades). 1 Hz never gets Ca²⁺ that high, but 900 small rises let calcineurin build up, and the EPSP shrinks. Spine B does not change. With APV nothing changes, yet the test EPSP itself is the same size: it runs through AMPA receptors.

Theory section (outline; each a toggle as on the other pages)

The spine as an RC circuit with a resistor in front

Lesson 2's membrane, three times over, joined by resistors. Current from the synapse charges the tiny head first (small C, so a big ΔV), then leaks through the neck into the dendrite and on to the soma, each step smoothing it. Note that the neck's real resistance is debated: estimates run from tens to hundreds of MΩ.

The Mg²⁺ block: a voltage-dependent plug

The curve mgFree(V) from the Synapse page, drawn: about 4 % free at −70 mV, half free near −20 mV. Why this makes the receptor a coincidence detector.

Two enzymes read the Ca²⁺: CaMKII and calcineurin

CaMKII needs a lot of Ca²⁺ and then adds AMPA receptors to the synapse. Calcineurin (a phosphatase) is switched on by a little Ca²⁺ and removes them. Why the same ion gives opposite results.

Early and late LTP

The first hour: existing receptors moved in. Hours later: new protein, a bigger spine head (one paragraph; not modelled).

Where these ideas come from

Bliss & Lømo (1973); Mg²⁺ block (Nowak et al., Mayer et al. 1984); Ca²⁺-level rule (Lisman 1989; Shouval, Bear & Cooper 2002); spine growth with LTP (Matsuzaki et al. 2004).
