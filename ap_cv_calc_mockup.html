Mockup: a conduction-velocity calculator to replace the Cable calculator on the action potential page.
  • The headline is the speed. λ and τ stay, but as the reasons, in the small print.
  • Speed against diameter (square, log–log): the bare axon's √d line against the myelinated axon's straight-d line, with real fibre types as boxes. The dotted line shows how wide a bare axon would have to be to keep up.
  • The trip: how long the spike takes to travel a distance you set. For example, stub your toe and the sharp pain arrives about a second before the burning one.
  • The constants here are fitted to the page's own simulation: 12 µm gives 80 m/s myelinated and 7 m/s bare, and 1 µm bare gives 2 m/s. The real version would take them from the model. The effect of the number of myelin wraps is a placeholder.

  • Speed against diameter: both axes go up in factors of 10. The dashed line is a bare axon: its speed grows with the square root of the diameter, so four times wider is only twice as fast. The solid line is a myelinated axon: twice as wide is twice as fast. The boxes are real fibre types, with their range of diameters and speeds. The dot is the axon you set.
  • The dotted line runs from your axon across to the bare line. Where it lands is how wide a bare axon would need to be to go as fast. For a 12 µm myelinated motor axon that is over a millimetre: that is why vertebrates myelinate, and why the squid made one giant axon instead.
  • The trip: how long each kind of fibre takes to carry a spike over the distance set.
